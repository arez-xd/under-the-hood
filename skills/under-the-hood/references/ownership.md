# Layer 2: who is behind it

You are about to give a company your money, your data or your workflow. Know who it is and how likely it is to be around.

## The legal entity

Read, and quote with the URL:

- the terms of service and privacy policy: "operated by", "the Company", the governing law and the address;
- the site footer and the imprint page (required in some countries);
- the checkout page and the receipt: the merchant name on the payment;
- the app store page: the developer or seller name.

Different names in different places are a finding: write them all.

## Age and stability

- **Domain age:** a public WHOIS or RDAP lookup for the registration date.
- **History:** the [Wayback Machine](https://web.archive.org/) for the landing and pricing pages. When did the product appear, how often did prices and plans change, did it used to be a different product?
- **Status page and changelog:** do they exist, how often is something shipped, how often is something down.
- **Contact:** a support address that answers, a real help centre, or a form only.

A young product is not a bad sign by itself. It means prices, features and terms will change; the verdict says so.

## Sibling products

Search the legal entity's name, and a distinctive sentence from the terms in quotes. Identical terms on several sites usually mean one operator running several products. Note them: they tell you how the company works (one product with depth, or many storefronts over the same backend), and their reviews apply to this one.

## Leftovers of other products

Signs the product was cloned or rebranded: another product's name in the page `<title>`, meta tags, emails, help-centre articles, error messages or asset paths; features from another product in the menu that don't belong here. Record each with where it was seen.

## What stays out

Only what the company publishes about itself. No personal data about employees beyond the founders the company names publicly, no digging into private profiles, no guessing identities.
